# AI for scientific discovery: How researchers use Claude

Anthropic (Economics team / The Anthropic Institute), **November 5, 2026**. The report studies how scientists actually use Claude to judge where AI is already accelerating discovery.

## Methods

- **207,049 consumer-plan sessions** (Aug 2–Sep 30, 2026): 128,914 on Claude (Chat/Cowork) and 78,135 on Claude Code, labeled by privacy-preserving classifiers using the SciNet taxonomy (7,262 tasks, 34 fields, 318 subfields).
- A survey of **528 scientists**, mostly life/health sciences, likely heavier AI users than typical.

## Key findings

- **Fields:** Formal sciences over-index. Computer science is **11.9%** of Claude sessions and **32.7%** of Claude Code sessions despite being 4.3% of researchers. Medicine and lab-based physical sciences under-use Claude.
- **Tasks:** Data analysis is **33.7%** of Claude sessions and **48.1%** of Claude Code sessions (vs. 27.6% of researcher time). Data gathering and ideation are underrepresented despite being ranked most important.
- **Specialized tools:** On Claude Science, over **70%** of sessions show high AI autonomy vs. 23.7% on Claude; ideation reaches ~8% of sessions.
- **Capability link:** Top-20%-capability tasks see **2–4x** the use expected from researcher time; bottom-20% (physical) tasks see very little — "scientists appear to use AI where it works best."

## Acceleration scenarios (illustrative)

- Current capabilities, widely adopted: AI could take on **27–52%** of research time (median 38%), implying **1.35x–2.02x** output.
- Full non-physical automation: CS and statistics could reach ~**15x** output; biology/chemistry/biomedical ~**3–4x** (physical work is the bottleneck).
- About **40%** of surveyed scientists expect AI to run full research projects alone by 2030, ~64% by 2035; 16% say never.

## Caveats

Adoption lags capability; physical experiments and question-selection are bottlenecks; cost assumptions ($30/AI-agent hour vs. $95/researcher hour) are illustrative; idea quality is not measured. Authors flag oversight, dangerous-research (especially biology), and career-impact risks.
